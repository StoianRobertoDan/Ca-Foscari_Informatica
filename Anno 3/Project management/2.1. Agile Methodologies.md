
Agile Methods:
- Are methods of software development that are geared to rapid software delivery and to avoid software failure.
- The software is developed and delivered in increments.
- Documentation processes and bureaucracy are minimized and code is prioritized.
	- Teams are usually self-directed and self-managed with the help and coaching of an agile project manager who also oversees the work.
- Principles:
	- Customer involvement.
	- Incremental delivery (followed by customer reviews).
	- Prioritized people over process.
	- Embrace change.
	- Maintain simplicity (with active work to eliminate complexity).
- Scaling:
	- Agile methods are meant for:
		- Small and medium sized projects.
		- Small co-located teams for improved communication.
		- New software development.
	- Difficulties:
		- Adapting the methods to larger, longer projects with multiple (and sometimes not co-located) teams.
		- Minimization of formal documentation:
			- Informality of agile methods are incompatible with the legal approach of larger companies.
			- Maintenance:
				- Majority of costs in a large company comes from the maintenance of existing software system, not from the software development.
				- Lack of importance on formal documentation can interfere with the agile approach to maintenance in large companies, especially if the original team can't be maintained.
		- Keeping customers involved in the development process.

Extreme Programming (XP):
- XP is an agile method that takes an ‘extreme’ approach to iterative development:
	- New versions may be built several times per day.
	- Increments are delivered to customers every 2 weeks.
	- All tests must be run for every build and the build is only accepted if tests run successfully.
- Principles and practices:
	- Incremental planning (requirements into stories and broken into tasks).
	- Small releases:
		- The minimal set of functionalities are developed first.
		- Frequent and incremental releases with added functionalities.
	- Simple design.
	- Test-first development:
		- An "automated unit test framework" is used to write tests for a functionality before its implemented.
	- Refactoring (to simplify code).
	- Pair programming.
	- Collective ownership:
		- All pairs work on all areas of the system.
		- No islands of expertise created.
		- All developers take responsibility of the code.
	- Continuous integration (of the completed tasks after tests are passed).
	- Sustainable pace (too much overtime decreases the code quality).
	- On-site customer (a representative of the end-user is part of the team).

Kanban:
- A scheduling system for lean manufacturing and just-in-time manufacturing.
- Usage of a physical or digital board for:
	- Visual representation of the work.
	- Limiting the work in progress.
- A Kanban board is used throughout the lifecycle of a project whereas a scrum board is cleared and recycled after each sprint.
- Kanban boards are more flexible with regards to tasks and timing. Tasks can be reprioritized, reassigned, or updated as needed.

Scrum:
- Scrum is an agile method that focuses on managing iterative development rather than specific agile practices.
- Phases:
	- Outline planning phase:
		- Where you establish the general objectives for the project and design the software architecture.
	- Series of sprint cycles.
	- closure phase:
		- Wraps up the project and completes required documentation.
- Division of work:
	- The product is broken down into a set of manageable and understandable chunks.
		- Unstable requirements do not hold up progress.
	- The whole team have visibility of everything and consequently team communication is improved.
	- Customers see on-time delivery of increments.
		- Trust between customers and developers is established.
- Terminology:
	- Development team:
		- Small self-organized group of software developers.
	- Scum meeting:
		- Short daily meeting usually with the whole team face-to-face that reviews progress and prioritizes work.
	- Scrum Master:
		- Responsible for ensuring the scrum processes are followed and coaching the team.
		- Responsible for interfacing with the rest of the company and ensuring the scrum team is not diverted from work.
		- ScrumMaster is not a project manager.
	- Sprint:
		- Short development iteration, usually 2-4 weeks.
	- Product increment:
		- The software increment that is delivered from a sprint, in finished state and ready to be shipped.
	- Product backlog:
		- An ordered list of everything that is known to be needed in the product.
		- A good backlog is “ DEEP ” 
			- Detailed appropriately.
			- Estimated.
			- Emergent.
			- Prioritized.
	- Product owner:
		- The person responsible for defining the product vision, managing the Product Backlog and ensuring the development team builds the right product.
		- Work closely with the stakeholders to deliver the maximum business value.
		- Makes sure that the needs of the customers and end-users are understood by the team.

	- Abnormal Sprint Termination:
		- Product Owner may call for Abnormal Sprint Termination (AST).
		- It is a business decision.
		- Typically something happened outside the team.
		- Rarely the team may ask PO to call AST .
		- Demo (if possible) and Retrospective are held as in normal sprint
- Stories:
	- Essential description of a desired functionality:
		- A short statement that captures who the user are, what they want and why they want it.
		- Structure:
			- As a...
			- I want...
			- So that...
		- Should be easy to turn into (automated) tests.
	- Stories incorporate in the sprint backlog with the addition of:
		- Related tasks 
		- Estimation: 
			- Task hours.
			- Task points.
			- Task count.
	- Effort:
		- Absolute estimates:
			- Stories are sized in term of “points” using the Fibonacci series.
		- Velocity:
			- After a sprint is complete an estimate of stories per sprint is done.
			- Velocity is NOT a performance measure.
	- Stories should be INVEST:
		- Independent.
		- Negotiable.
		- Valuable.
		- Estimable.
		- Small.
		- Testable.
	- Epic:
		- Too large of a story and should be broken down in smaller stories.
- Boards:
	- Visual boards of stories divided between what should do, what is doing and what is done.
- Burn-down charts:
	- Residual work units.
- Burn-up charts:
	- Completed work units.
