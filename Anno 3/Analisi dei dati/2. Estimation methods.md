**Method of Moments**
 - **Moments**:
	- The k-th population moment (the parameter):
		- $\mu_{k}=\mathbb{E}(X^k)$
	- The k-th sample moment (the statistic):
		- $M_{k}=\frac{1}{n}\sum\limits^{n}_{i=1}X^{k}_{i}$
			- $M_{k}$ is an estimator of $\mu_{k}$
		- $m_{k}=\frac{1}{n}\sum\limits^{n}_{i=1}X^{k}_{i}$
			- $m_{k}$ is an estimate of $\mu_k$
	- The **first moment** is the **mean**:
		- The first population moment is the [[3. Variabili casuali#^b93ced|population mean]] ($\mu_{1}=\mu$).
		- The first sample moment is the [[1.1. Statistics#^e4730f|sample mean]] ($M_{1}=\bar{X}$ and $m_{1}=\bar{x}$).
 - **Central Moments** (centralizing the data by subtracting the sample mean):
	- The k-th population central moment with $k \geq 2$ (the parameter):
		- $\mu'_{k}=\mathbb{E}(X-\mu_{1})^k$
	- The k-th sample central moment (the statistic):
		- $M'_{k}=\frac{1}{n}\sum\limits^{n}_{i=1}(X_{i}-\bar{X})^{k}$
			- $M'_{k}$ is an estimator of $\mu'_{k}$
		- $m'_{k}=\frac{1}{n}\sum\limits^{n}_{i=1}(x_{i}-\bar{x})^{k}$
			- $m'_{k}$ is an estimate of $\mu'_k$
	- The **second central moment** is the **variance**:
		- The second population central moment is t 1he [[3. Variabili casuali#^b32abc|variance]] ($\mu'_{2}=\sigma^2$).
		- The second sample central moment is [[1.1. Statistics#^c5af69|sample variance]] (but the denominator is now $n$ and not $n-1$): $M'_{2}=S^{2}(\frac{n-1}{n})=\frac{1}{n}\sum\limits^{n}_{i=1}(X_{i}-\bar{X})^{2}$ 
 - Estimation:
	- Estimation through method of moments is used to choose a distribution whose properties are close to the properties of our data.
	- To estimate k parameters, equate the first k population and sample moments:
		- $\left\{ \begin{array}{ccc} \mu_{1}(\theta) & = & M_{1} \\ ... & ... \\ \mu_{k}(\theta) & = & M_{k}\end{array}\right.$
	- Depending on the context, one may use raw moments or central moments. 
		- It is also possible to combine raw and central moments when convenient.

**Method of Maximum Likelihood** ^275866
 - Idea:
	- Since the sample $X = (X_{1}, . . . , X_{n})$ has already been observed, we find such parameters that maximize the probability (likelihood) for this to happen. In other words, we make the event that has already happened to be as likely as possible.
 - Definition:
	- **Maximum likelihood estimator** is the parameter value that maximizes the likelihood of the observed sample.
		- For a **discrete distribution**, we maximize the joint pmf (probability mass function) of data $P(X_{1}, . . . , X_{n})$.
		- For a **continuous distribution**, we maximize the joint density $f(X_{1}, . . . , X_{n})$.
	- Discrete distribution:
		- The probability of a given sample is the joint pmf of (i.i.d.) data: $$ \mathbb{P} \{X=(X_{1}, . . . , X_{n})\} = \mathbb{P}(X_{1}, . . . , X_{n}) = \prod_{i=1}^{n}\mathbb{P}(X_{i})$$
		- Is usually noted as $L(\theta)$.
	- Continuous distribution:
		- In a continuous distribution the probability to observe a specific case is 0 by definition. Instead, the method of maximum likelihood will maximize the probability of observing a close range (for a very small h): $$\mathbb{P}\{x-h < X < x+h\}= \int_{x-h}^{x+h}f(y)dy\approx (2h)f(x)$$
		- Sometimes the likelihood has no critical points inside its domain, then it is maximized at the boundary.
 - **Log-likelihood** ($\ell(\theta) = \ln(L(\theta))$):
	- To maximize the likelihood, we consider the critical points by taking derivatives with respect to all unknown parameters and equating them to 0.
		- Because natural logarithm is a **strictly increasing function**:
			- Wherever $L(\theta)$ peaks, $\ln(L(\theta))$ peaks at exactly the same $\theta$**. 
			- The transformation preserves the location of the maximum (maximized by exactly the same parameter).
		- $L(\theta)$ have smaller values that can cause numerical underflow.
	- Since differentiating the sum is easier than differentiating the product:
		- Discrete case: $$\ln \prod_{i=1}^{n}\mathbb{P}(X_{i}) = \sum\limits_{i=1}^{n}\ln \mathbb{P}(X_{i})$$
		- Continuous case: $$\ln \prod_{i=1}^{n}f(X_{i}) = \sum\limits_{i=1}^{n}\ln f(X_{i})$$
 - **Regularity Conditions**:
	- Must be a **regular** estimation problem.
		- The support of the random variable does not depend on the model parameter.
	- The derivatives of appropriate order exist and are finite.
		- The first derivative of the log-likelihood is called the **score function**.
		- Likelihood equation: $\frac{d\ell(\hat{\theta})}{d\theta}=0$
			- $\hat{\theta}$ is a maximum if $\frac{d^{2}\ell(\hat{\theta})}{d\theta^{2}}<0$
 - **Asymptotic Properties**:
	- Asymptotically unbiased:
		- $Bias(\widehat{\theta}) = \mathbb{E}(\widehat{\theta}) - \theta \rightarrow 0$  for $n\rightarrow\infty$ 
	- [[1. Population, sample and notations#^4b93d5|Consistent]]:
		- $\hat{\theta}\rightarrow\theta$ for $n\rightarrow\infty$
	- Asymptotically normal: ^2ea646
		- $\hat{\theta}\approx N(\theta,\mathcal{I}(\theta)^{-1})$ for large $n$
		- $\mathcal{I}(\theta)$ is the [[2. Estimation methods#^c1cff4 |fisher information]].
- Invariance:
	- Definition:
		- If $\hat{\theta}$ is the maximum likelihood estimator of $\theta$, then $g(\hat{\theta})$ is the maximum likelihood estimator of $g(\theta) = \psi$.
	- Invariance doesn't ensure unbiasness.

**Estimation of Standard Errors**:
 - Standard errors are used to measure the [[1. Population, sample and notations#^df2f06|precision]] of estimators. To estimate them, we differentiate an expression for the standard error and estimate all the unknown parameters in it.
 - **Fisher information**: ^c1cff4
	- Definition: $$\mathcal{I}(\theta) = \mathbb{E}\left\{-\frac{d^{2}\ell(\hat{\theta})}{d\theta^{2}}\right\} = \mathbb{E}\left\{-\frac{d^{2}}{d\theta^{2}}\sum\limits_{i=1}^{n}ln f(X_{i})\right\} = \sum\limits_{i=1}^{n}\mathbb{E}\left\{-\frac{d^{2}}{d\theta^{2}}\ln f(X_{i})\right\}$$
		- For a simple random sample: $$\mathcal{I}(\theta) = n\mathbb{E}\left\{-\frac{d^{2}}{d\theta^{2}}\ln f(X_{1})\right\}$$
	- **Observed information**: $$J(\theta) = -\ell''(\theta) = \sum\limits_{i=1}^{n}\left\{-\frac{d^{2}}{d\theta^{2}}ln f(X_{i})\right\} = \sum\limits_{i=1}^{n}g(X_{i})$$
		- By the Law of Large Numbers, as $n\rightarrow\infty$: $$g(X) = \frac{J(\theta)}{n}\rightarrow\mathbb{E}\left\{g(X)\right\} = \mathbb{E}\left\{-\frac{d^{2}}{d\theta^{2}}\ln f(X)\right\} \Rightarrow J(\theta)\approx\mathcal{I}(\theta)$$
	- Difference:
		- $\mathcal{I}(\theta)$ is an average over repeated samples.
		- $J(\theta)$ reflects the information in the observed sample.
 - Standard Error of Maximum Likelihood Estimator:
	- Since the maximum likelihood estimator for large samples with regularity conditions is [[2. Estimation methods#^2ea646|asymptotical normal]], the standard error of the maximum likelihood estimator ($\hat{\theta}$) is asymptotically: $SE(\hat{\theta})\approx\mathcal{I}(\theta)^{-\frac{1}{2}}$.
		- The estimate of the standard errors are:
			- $\hat{SE}(\hat{\theta})\approx\mathcal{I}(\hat{\theta})^{-\frac{1}{2}}$
			- $\hat{SE}(\hat{\theta})\approx J(\hat{\theta})^{-\frac{1}{2}}$
 - **Cramér-Rao inequity**: ^3d5b8a
	- Gives a lower bound for the variance of an unbiased estimator $\hat{\theta}$: $Var(\hat{\theta})\geq\mathcal{I}(\hat{\theta})^{-1}$
	- If an unbiased estimator achieves this bound is called **efficient**.
		- The maximum likelihood estimator is efficient.

Vector parameter:
 - Multidimensional parameter is $\theta = \left(\begin{array}{c}\theta_{1} \\ \vdots \\ \theta_{k}\end{array}\right)$ and its estimator is $\hat{\theta} = \left(\begin{array}{c}\hat{\theta}_{1} \\ \vdots \\ \hat{\theta}_{k}\end{array}\right)$ 
	 - Each $\hat{\theta}_{n}$ is a random variable
 - Moments: 
	 - Mean vector: $\mu = \left(\begin{array}{c}\mu_{1} \\ \vdots \\ \mu_{k}\end{array}\right)$ 
	 - Variance-covariance matrix: $\Sigma = \left(\begin{array}{cccc}\sigma_{1}^{2} & \sigma_{12} & \ldots & \sigma_{1k} \\ \vdots & \vdots & \ddots & \vdots \\ \sigma_{k1} & \sigma_{k2} & \ldots & \sigma_{k}^{2}\end{array}\right)$ with $\sigma_{rs} = Cov[\hat{\theta}_{r}, \hat{\theta}_{s}]$
		 - Covariance:
			 - $$Cov[X,Y] = \mathbb{E}[(X-\mu_{X})(Y-\mu_{Y})] = \mathbb{E}[(X-\mathbb{E}[X])(Y-\mathbb{E}[Y])]$$
			 - $Cov[X,Y] = \mathbb{E}[XY] - \mathbb{E}[X]\mathbb{E}[Y]$
				 - Like in $Var[X] =  \mathbb{E}[X^{2}]-[\mathbb{E}[X]]^{2}$
				 - $Cov[X,X] = \mathbb{E}[XX] - \mathbb{E}[X]\mathbb{E}[X] = \mathbb{E}[X^{2}]-[\mathbb{E}[X]]^{2} = Var[X]$
			 - Sign of covariance:
				 - If $Cov[X,Y] < 0$ then $X$ and $Y$ tend to move in the **same direction**
			     - If $Cov[X,Y] < 0$ then $X$ and $Y$ tend to move in **opposite directions**
			     - If $Cov[X,Y] = 0$ then there is no **linear** relationship (but could still be dependent nonlinearly)
			 - Since $Cov[X_{r},X_{s}] = Cov[X_{s},X_{r}]$, the matrix is always **symmetric**.
 - Maximum likelihood estimator:
	- With a vector parameter you take partial derivatives with respect to each component and set all of them to zero simultaneously (Score equation):
		- $\frac{\partial\ell(\theta)}{\partial\theta_{1}}, \;\; \ldots, \;\; \frac{\partial\ell(\theta)}{\partial\theta_{k}}$
		- This is also noted as: $\nabla_{\theta}\ell(\theta) = 0$
		- $\partial$ (partial) is used instead of $d$ since the derivatives are partial (differentiate $\ell$  with respect to one of the variables​ while **treating all other variables as constants**)
	- Hessian Matrix: $H(\theta) = \left(\begin{array}{cccc}\frac{\partial^{2}\ell(\theta)}{\partial\theta_{1}^{2}} & \frac{\partial^{2}\ell(\theta)}{\partial\theta_{1}\partial\theta_{2}} & \ldots & \frac{\partial^{2}\ell(\theta)}{\partial\theta_{1}\partial\theta_{k}} \\ \vdots & \vdots & \ddots & \vdots \\ \frac{\partial^{2}\ell(\theta)}{\partial\theta_{k}\partial\theta_{1}} & \frac{\partial^{2}\ell(\theta)}{\partial\theta_{k}\partial\theta_{2}} & \ldots & \frac{\partial^{2}\ell(\theta)}{\partial\theta_{k}^{2}}\end{array}\right)$
		- This is also noted as: $\nabla_{\theta}^{2}\ell(\theta)$
	- Fisher Information is a matrix: 
		- $\mathcal{I}(\theta)_{r,s} = H(\theta) = -\mathbb{E}\left[\frac{\partial^{2}\ell(\theta)}{\partial\theta_{r}\partial\theta_{s}}\right]$
			- Its diagonal entries $\mathcal{I}_{r,s}$ play the role of the scalar Fisher information for each component $\theta_{r,r}$, and the off-diagonal entries capture how much information about $\theta_{r}$ is shared with $\theta_{s}$.
		- Observed information (matrix):
			- $J(\theta)_{r,s} = -\ell''(\theta) = -H(\theta) = -\frac{\partial^{2}\ell(\theta)}{\partial\theta_{r}\partial\theta_{s}}$
	- Cramér-Rao inequity:
		- $Cov(\hat{\theta}) \geq\mathcal{I}(\theta)^{-1}$
